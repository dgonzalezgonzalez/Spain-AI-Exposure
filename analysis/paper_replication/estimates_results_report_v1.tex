\subsection{Baseline Results}
\label{sec:v1_baseline}

Figure~\ref{fig:v1_descriptive} describes the evolution of the two labor-market outcomes before turning to the regression evidence. Registered unemployment falls in all three exposure groups, but the decline becomes slower among highly exposed occupations after November 2022. The separation is gradual rather than immediate, as one would expect if firms adopted generative AI over time. New contract registrations are much more volatile and show no stable ordering by exposure. The similar pre-release paths are reassuring, but they are not sufficient for identification because exposure groups may differ in their recovery from the pandemic and in other occupation-specific shocks.

The preferred specification asks whether outcomes diverged after ChatGPT's release across occupations with different exposure, while comparing occupations within the same broad CNO1 family in every month. This restriction is economically important: it removes shocks shared by related occupations, such as changes in sectoral demand or the pace of the post-pandemic recovery, without requiring those shocks to follow a parametric trend.\footnote{For CNO4 occupation \(j\), CNO1 family \(g(j)\), and event time \(k\), the event-study regression is \(Y_{jt}=\alpha_j+\lambda_{g(j),t}+\sum_{k\neq -1}\beta_k(A_j/0.10)\mathbf{1}\{t-T_0=k\}+\varepsilon_{jt}\). The event window runs from \(-21\) to \(40\), November 2022 is \(T_0\), and October 2022 is omitted. Occupation fixed effects absorb permanent differences across CNO4 occupations, CNO1-by-month fixed effects absorb shocks common to occupations in the same broad family, and standard errors are clustered by CNO4.}

Figure~\ref{fig:v1_eventstudy} reports the preferred dynamic estimates. In panel (a), the unemployment gradient is close to zero around the release date and becomes positive only after several months. A 10 percentage-point increase in exposure is associated with roughly 1--2 percent more registered unemployment over much of the post-treatment period. The estimates remain positive near the end of the sample, but their confidence intervals widen and frequently include zero. Panel (b) shows no comparable persistence in new contracts: the coefficients fluctuate around zero and are imprecisely estimated. Taken together, the panels are consistent with a gradual increase in the stock of registered unemployment rather than a discrete contraction in the monthly flow of hiring. They do not, by themselves, distinguish displacement from slower exits out of unemployment.

The pre-treatment evidence warrants caution. For registered unemployment, the joint-nullity and flatness tests reject over event times \(-21\) through \(-2\) (both \(p=0.003\)), but not over the more recent window \(-10\) through \(-2\) (\(p=0.367\) and \(0.280\)). The early window accounts for the full-period rejection. For new contracts, the corresponding tests reject over the full window (\(p=0.032\) and \(0.045\)) and are borderline over the recent window (\(p=0.049\) and \(0.035\)). Allowing errors to be correlated within CNO3 groups does not overturn the broad conclusion. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:v1_pretrends} reports all windows and clustering choices. The CNO1-by-month effects improve the economic comparability of occupations, especially in the period closest to treatment, but they do not eliminate the possibility that differential recovery paths contaminate the post-treatment gradient. We therefore interpret the estimates as disciplined but still assumption-dependent evidence.

Table~\ref{tab:v1_main_effects} summarizes the post-treatment path in two intervals. The adjustment period covers November 2022 through November 2024, when firms were learning about and beginning to deploy generative AI. The later period covers December 2024 through March 2026, when adoption had more time to affect staffing and job search.\footnote{The table estimates \(Y_{jt}=\alpha_j+\lambda_{g(j),t}+\beta_A(A_j/0.10)\mathbf{1}\{0\leq e_t\leq24\}+\beta_L(A_j/0.10)\mathbf{1}\{25\leq e_t\leq40\}+\varepsilon_{jt}\), where \(\lambda_{g(j),t}\) denotes common month effects or CNO1-by-month effects. All pre-treatment months form the omitted category. The table coefficients compare each interval with the average pre-treatment exposure gradient; Figure~\ref{fig:v1_eventstudy} instead normalizes each monthly coefficient to October 2022.}

The benchmark in column 1 implies 1.9 percent more registered unemployment during the adjustment period and 3.8 percent more in the later period for a 10 percentage-point increase in exposure. Restricting identification to within-CNO1 comparisons attenuates the estimates to 1.2 and 1.7 percent in column 2. The adjustment-period coefficient is statistically significant at the 5 percent level; the later coefficient is positive but less precisely estimated. The hypothesis of equal effects across the two intervals cannot be rejected (\(p=0.486\)). Clustering at CNO3 in column 3 leaves the point estimates unchanged and modestly increases the standard errors. The attenuation after adding CNO1-by-month effects is economically informative: part of the unconditional relationship reflects shocks shared by broad occupational families rather than variation between comparable CNO4 occupations.

The contract estimates display a similar attenuation. The benchmark coefficients in column 4 are 3.3 and 2.9 percent, while the preferred estimates in column 5 are 1.4 and 1.3 percent. Neither preferred estimate is statistically distinguishable from zero, and the two intervals are nearly identical (\(p=0.807\)). The baseline evidence therefore points to a modest increase in registered unemployment in more exposed occupations, but the effect is estimated imprecisely and is not accompanied by a persistent aggregate response in new contracts.

The absence of a CNO4 labor-force denominator prevents a direct occupational unemployment rate from being constructed. The estimates should therefore be interpreted as effects on registered unemployment outcomes rather than as effects on unemployment rates. This limitation does not invalidate the within-CNO4 fixed-effects design, but it prevents the results from being expressed as changes in the share of the labor force that is unemployed.

\subsubsection{Robustness Checks}
\label{sec:v1_robustness}

Appendix Table~\ref{tab:v1_robustness} examines whether the baseline result is an artifact of outcome coding or exposure construction. Replacing \(\log(Y)\) with \(\log(Y+1)\) leaves the preferred unemployment estimates at 1.2 percent in the adjustment period and 1.7 percent later. Cosine-weighted exposure produces somewhat larger estimates of 1.8 and 2.9 percent. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:v1_jev_robustness} applies the same long-difference specifications to three Jev-based exposure measures. Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:v1_exposure_correlations} compares the occupation rankings across measures. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:v1_robustness_pretrends} and Figures~\ref{fig:v1_robust_u}--\ref{fig:v1_jev_robust_c} report the corresponding event-study diagnostics.

The binary design in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:v1_binary_treatment} classifies occupations above the 75th-percentile cutoff, 0.1169, as treated and retains all remaining occupations as controls. The preferred specification yields 3.9 percent more registered unemployment during the adjustment period and 5.8 percent more later, although the later estimate is imprecise and the two interval effects are not statistically different. Contract estimates remain close to zero. The larger binary coefficients are consistent with concentration of the response in the upper exposure tail, but discretization discards dose information and changes the estimand. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:v1_binary_pretrends} and Figure~\ref{fig:v1_binary_event} report the corresponding diagnostics.

Appendix Table~\ref{tab:v1_province} disaggregates the panel by province and absorbs province-specific monthly shocks. The preferred unemployment estimates are 0.9 percent during the adjustment period and 1.0 percent later; only the former is marginally distinguishable from zero. Contract estimates are approximately 0.9 percent in both intervals and are imprecise. The smaller coefficients suggest that regional composition contributes to the aggregate gradient, although the direction remains positive. Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:v1_province_event} reports the monthly estimates.

No single CNO1 family drives the result: the leave-one-family-out estimates in Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:v1_leave_one_out} remain positive and close to the full-sample estimate. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:v1_trailing_contracts} and Figure~\ref{fig:v1_trailing_contracts} replace monthly contracts with registrations accumulated over the preceding 12 months. The preferred estimates, 1.5 percent during adjustment and 0.9 percent later, are not statistically different from zero. This measure smooths hiring flows but does not create a stock of active contracts, so it is a robustness check rather than a substitute outcome.

\subsection{Heterogeneity}
\label{sec:v1_heterogeneity}

\input{figuresNtables/heterogeneity_v1.tex}

Table~\ref{tab:v1_heterogeneity} shows that the average response masks economically relevant differences by age. In the preferred within-CNO1 specification, a 10 percentage-point increase in exposure is associated with 1.5 percent more registered unemployment for workers under 30, 1.7 percent for ages 30--39, and 0.9 percent for workers age 40 or older during the adjustment period. The later-period estimates are 2.2, 3.4, and 1.2 percent. The estimate for ages 30--39 is individually significant in the later period, but separate significance levels do not establish that effects differ across groups. The pooled tests in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:v1_age_cross_group_tests} do not reject equality across all three groups (\(p=0.105\)); they do reject equality between ages 30--39 and age 40 or older (\(p=0.035\)), but not between ages 30--39 and workers under 30 (\(p=0.320\)). The age concentration is therefore suggestive rather than definitive.

The contract results provide a complementary view of adjustment. For workers under 30, the preferred coefficients are 2.2 percent during adjustment and 2.6 percent later, both statistically significant. The corresponding estimates for ages 30--39 are 1.3 and 1.7 percent, while those for workers age 40 or older are close to zero. The pooled tests reject equality across age groups in both periods. Younger workers may therefore experience more labor-market turnover, while the 30--39 group exhibits the clearest later accumulation in registered unemployment. The data do not observe worker transitions, however, so this interpretation cannot distinguish job creation, repeated short contracts, or slower exits from unemployment.

The age results should be interpreted as heterogeneity in occupation-by-age cells, rather than as evidence that individual workers in one age group are causally displaced. Early-to-mid-career workers may occupy tasks and career positions that are more sensitive to organizational adoption, but the present data cannot distinguish this mechanism from differences in job mobility, hiring, or unemployment duration. The pooled tests and subgroup pre-trend diagnostics therefore make the age pattern suggestive rather than definitive. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:v1_heterogeneity_pretrends} and Figures~\ref{fig:v1_heterogeneity_u}--\ref{fig:v1_heterogeneity_c} report the subgroup diagnostics and dynamic paths.\footnote{SEPE omits its CNO4-by-age breakdown for May 2024. We recover uniquely identified integer cells by inverting each June level and the corresponding June-over-May percentage change, with the observed May CNO4 total used as an adding-up validation. Cells that are not uniquely identified remain missing.}

\subsection{Heterogeneity by Occupational Feminization}
\label{sec:v1_feminization}

The occupational-feminization analysis asks whether exposure has different consequences in occupations with historically different gender composition. The predetermined index is the female share of EPA employment in the corresponding CNO2 group, averaged over 2017--2019. We split occupations at its median, 0.303, and allow the exposure gradient to differ on either side of that threshold within the preferred specification. This is an occupation-level task-composition exercise; it does not estimate different effects for individual men and women.\footnote{The estimating equation is \(\ln Y_{jt}=\alpha_j+\lambda_{g(j),t}+\sum_{p\in\{A,L\}}\sum_{h\in\{B,H\}}\beta_{ph}(A_j/0.10)\mathbf{1}\{e_t\in p\}\mathbf{1}\{F_j\in h\}+\varepsilon_{jt}\), where \(A\) and \(L\) denote the adjustment and later periods, \(F_j\) is the predetermined feminization index, and the pre-treatment period is omitted. Standard errors are clustered by CNO4.}

\input{figuresNtables/feminization_median_split_v1.tex}

Table~\ref{tab:v1_feminization_median_split} reports one regression for each outcome. Below-median occupations have unemployment gradients of 4.3 percent during adjustment and 7.7 percent later. The corresponding estimates for more feminized occupations are close to zero or negative and imprecise. New-contract estimates are small in both groups. This pattern is consistent with larger displacement or slower unemployment exits in less feminized occupations, but it is not robust enough to establish a mechanism: the subgroup pre-trend tests reject for part of the sample, and detrending materially changes the dynamic paths reported in Appendix~\ref{app:v1_feminization}.

\subsection{Further Results}
\label{sec:v1_further}

This subsection asks whether the main conclusion survives estimators that construct the counterfactual differently. Both alternatives are informative, but neither reproduces the preferred within-CNO1 comparison while retaining a continuous treatment.

\subsubsection{Continuous-DiD Alternative}
\label{sec:v1_contdid}

The public \texttt{contdid} implementation does not admit the CNO1-by-month effects used in the preferred design. We therefore report three transparent approximations instead of treating residualization as an equivalent conditional estimator. The CNO1-stratified estimator applies continuous DiD separately within families with at least 15 positive-exposure and 5 zero-exposure occupations and aggregates family-specific average causal responses using positive-exposure occupation shares. It retains CNO1 groups 1--5 and about 88 percent of positive-exposure occupations. A second estimator subtracts the change among zero-exposure occupations in each supported family before pooling. The third is the unconditional continuous-DiD comparison.

Appendix Table~\ref{tab:v1_contdid_alternatives} reports average causal response on the treated (ACRT) estimates over the same two intervals.\footnote{For a post-treatment window \(W\), the estimand is \(\widehat{\theta}_{W}=|W|^{-1}\sum_{t\in W}\widehat{\mathrm{ACRT}}_{t}\). If \(a_W\) assigns weight \(1/|W|\) to event times in \(W\), the estimated variance is \(a_W'\widehat{\Sigma}a_W\), where \(\widehat{\Sigma}\) is the influence-function covariance matrix. Family-specific estimates are aggregated using positive-exposure occupation shares, and disjoint-family covariance matrices enter with squared aggregation weights.} The CNO1-stratified unemployment response rises from 3.4 percent during adjustment to 7.1 percent later. The contract estimates are 2.1 and 5.7 percent. These effects are larger than the TWFE estimates and reinforce the direction of the unemployment result. They remain secondary because stratification cannot absorb family-specific shocks in every month and because the full-period pre-treatment diagnostics are less favorable. Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:v1_contdid_paths} reports the dynamic paths.

\subsubsection{Synthetic Difference-in-Differences}
\label{sec:v1_sdid}

Synthetic difference-in-differences provides a separate upper-tail design. Occupations with exposure above 0.1169 are treated, while every occupation at or below the cutoff remains eligible for the donor pool. Retaining middle-exposure occupations makes the donor pool more economically comparable than a zero-exposure-only design, but it changes the estimand from a marginal response to an upper-tail contrast.\footnote{Conceptually, \(\widehat{\tau}_{SDID}=T_1^{-1}\sum_{t>T_0}\left(\overline{Y}_{Tt}-\sum_j\widehat{\omega}_jY_{jt}\right)-\sum_{t\leq T_0}\widehat{\lambda}_t\left(\overline{Y}_{Tt}-\sum_j\widehat{\omega}_jY_{jt}\right)\), where \(\widehat{\omega}_j\) are donor weights and \(\widehat{\lambda}_t\) are pre-period time weights.}

Unit weights reproduce the treated pre-period path, and time weights emphasize pre-treatment months that best predict post-treatment donor outcomes. The adjusted variant removes CNO1-by-month means before constructing the synthetic comparison.\footnote{Inference uses 500 placebo reassignments. The CNO1-by-month projection is computed from outcome and family-month cells and is held fixed across placebo assignments. Event-study confidence intervals use the same number of reassignments.}

Appendix Table~\ref{tab:v1_sdid} reports a 2.7 percent unemployment effect during adjustment and 4.1 percent later; the corresponding contract estimates are near zero. Synthetic weighting improves pre-treatment fit and again points to a positive unemployment response. Its stronger fit comes at the cost of imposing an ad hoc binary treatment and estimating a different parameter. Appendix~\ref{app:v1_sdid} reports the paths, donor weights, and concentration diagnostics.

\subsection{Summary of Evidence}
\label{sec:v1_synthesis}

Across designs, more exposed occupations tend to experience a relative increase in registered unemployment after the release of ChatGPT. The preferred within-family TWFE estimates are economically modest and imprecise, while continuous DiD and synthetic DiD produce larger positive effects under different identifying assumptions. New contracts do not show a stable aggregate response, although younger workers exhibit more positive contract gradients. The age evidence points most clearly to workers ages 30--39 in the later period, but formal cross-group tests make this conclusion suggestive rather than definitive. The full-period pre-trend rejections and the absence of a CNO4 labor-force denominator prevent a strong causal claim. The evidence is therefore consistent with gradual AI-related labor-market adjustment, concentrated in some worker and occupation groups, but does not establish that AI penetration has raised unemployment throughout the Spanish labor market.
